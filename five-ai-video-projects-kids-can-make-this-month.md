# Five AI Video Projects Kids Can Make This Month

*Simple creative challenges that teach storytelling, planning, and responsible AI use.*

**Lux Automaton Editorial · July 16, 2026 · 5 min read**

![Ace, LANA, and Dr. Torrey Dooley introduce five creative AI video projects for kids.](01-thumbnail-five-ai-video-projects.png)

## In this story

01. Start with a storyboard  
02. Use short scenes  
03. Explain what the AI helped create

AI video becomes less mysterious when kids build in small scenes. One character, one setting, one action, and one feeling are enough for a strong first project.

Try a neighborhood helper robot, a weather report from the future, a kindness commercial, a science explainer, or a mini adventure where a character solves a real problem.

The important lesson is not just the final video. Students learn to plan, revise, credit tools, check facts, protect privacy, and explain their choices.

> **Ace asks:** “What do you think your story should help someone understand, feel, or do?”

## 01. Start with a storyboard

![Ace and LANA show kids how to plan a character, setting, action, and ending before creating an AI video.](02-photo-start-with-a-storyboard.png)

A storyboard is a simple map of the video. Kids do not need advanced drawing skills. Four boxes are enough:

1. **Character:** Who is the story about?
2. **Setting:** Where does the scene happen?
3. **Action:** What does the character do?
4. **Ending:** What changes or gets solved?

Planning first helps children make creative decisions before a tool starts generating options. It also makes it easier to notice when an AI result does not match the original idea.

A strong child-friendly prompt can follow this pattern:

> “Create a short scene with **one character**, in **one setting**, doing **one clear action**, with a **specific feeling**. Keep the design consistent with the previous scene.”

Before a child uploads a photo, voice recording, or personal story, a trusted adult should review the tool’s privacy rules and age requirements. Use original characters whenever possible, and ask permission before including another person’s face, voice, name, artwork, or experience.

### Project 1: Neighborhood Helper Robot

Create a friendly robot that notices a small community problem and helps solve it.

A four-scene plan could be:

- The robot sees litter near a playground.
- It asks neighbors to help.
- Everyone cleans the area together.
- The robot celebrates the teamwork—not itself.

**Skills practiced:** empathy, cause and effect, character motivation, and community thinking.

**Responsible AI checkpoint:** Do not include a real address, school name, or identifiable neighborhood details. Keep the setting fictional or general.

### Project 2: Weather Report From the Future

Invite kids to become a weather reporter in the year 2050. What might the studio look like? What tools could forecasters use? How might communities prepare for heat, rain, wind, or snow?

Children can research real weather terms first, then clearly label futuristic predictions as imagination.

**Skills practiced:** research, presentation, world-building, and separating facts from fiction.

**Responsible AI checkpoint:** Check weather and climate claims with trusted sources. A creative prediction should never be presented as a confirmed fact.

### Project 3: Kindness Commercial

Make a 15–30 second message that inspires one simple action: invite someone to join a game, thank a helper, include a new student, protect a shared space, or speak respectfully online.

A commercial needs a clear audience, message, and call to action. Ask kids to finish this sentence:

> “After watching my video, I hope people will…”

**Skills practiced:** persuasive writing, tone, visual design, and audience awareness.

**Responsible AI checkpoint:** Use original characters or people who gave permission to participate. Kindness content should never embarrass someone or reveal a private situation.

### Project 4: Science in 60 Seconds

Choose one focused question: Why do leaves change color? How does a battery power a toy? Why do we wash our hands? What makes a rainbow?

Build the explanation with a beginning, one main idea, and a simple recap. Kids can ask AI to suggest examples or visual metaphors, but they should verify every important fact before recording.

**Skills practiced:** research, simplification, sequencing, and teaching.

**Responsible AI checkpoint:** For health or medical topics, AI is a learning assistant—not a doctor or nurse. As Dr. Torrey Dooley says, “Let’s keep it real: check the facts, use trusted health sources, and ask a qualified professional when a real health decision is involved.”

### Project 5: Mini Adventure—Solve It!

Create a character with a goal, an obstacle, and a helpful idea. The problem can be small: finding a lost library book, helping a robot cross a puddle, saving a garden from a dry week, or building a quieter study space.

The most interesting ending is not always “the hero wins.” Sometimes the character asks for help, changes the plan, or learns from a mistake.

**Skills practiced:** problem-solving, revision, emotional growth, and story structure.

**Responsible AI checkpoint:** Avoid copying a famous character or asking AI to imitate a living artist. Original worlds give children more room to develop their own voices.

## 02. Use short scenes

![Ace demonstrates how four short clips can become a clear, consistent AI video.](03-photo-use-short-scenes.png)

Long prompts often ask the AI to manage too many details at once. Short scenes are easier to plan, generate, review, and replace.

For a first project, try **three to five clips of about five to eight seconds each**. Give every clip one job.

A useful scene formula is:

> **Character + setting + action + feeling + camera view**

For example: “The small silver helper robot stands beside a community garden, carefully waters one sunflower, feels proud, medium camera shot.”

When the next scene begins, repeat the important character details. Kids can keep a “character recipe” with clothing, colors, size, personality, and special features. This improves continuity and teaches careful observation.

After each clip, pause and ask:

- Does it match the storyboard?
- Is the action easy to understand?
- Did the character stay consistent?
- Is anything strange, unsafe, or misleading?
- Should we keep it, revise it, or try again?

Revision is not a failure. It is the work of directing.

## 03. Explain what the AI helped create

![Dr. Torrey Dooley and LANA demonstrate a clear AI-assistance credit for a student video.](04-photo-explain-what-ai-helped-create.png)

A responsible creator can explain where the idea came from, what the AI generated, and what the human changed.

Kids can use this simple credit:

> “I created the idea and storyboard. I used an AI tool to help make visual options. I selected the clips, changed the script, checked the facts, and completed the final edit.”

That statement protects the child’s authorship while giving honest credit to the tool.

A project credit card might include:

- **Story idea:** Me
- **Storyboard:** Me, with feedback from a parent, teacher, Ace, or LANA
- **Images or clips:** AI-assisted
- **Facts:** Checked with trusted sources
- **Voice:** My narration or a properly licensed voice
- **Final decisions and edit:** Me

LANA’s reminder is simple: AI can suggest, generate, and organize. People still choose the purpose, judge the result, protect others, and decide what should be shared.

Before publishing, families can complete one final review:

- We kept personal information private.
- Everyone shown or heard gave permission.
- We checked important facts.
- We used original or properly permitted material.
- We explained how AI helped.
- The video is kind, useful, and appropriate for its audience.

## A one-month LUX AI Kids challenge

Use the five projects as a flexible family or classroom challenge:

- **Week 1:** Storyboard the Neighborhood Helper Robot.
- **Week 2:** Create the Future Weather Report and Kindness Commercial.
- **Week 3:** Research and produce Science in 60 Seconds.
- **Week 4:** Build the Mini Adventure, then host a small showcase.

During the showcase, ask each creator three questions:

1. What decision are you most proud of?
2. What did the AI help you create?
3. What did you change, verify, or improve yourself?

These questions shift attention away from flashy generation and toward learning, judgment, and creative ownership.

## The real project is becoming a thoughtful creator

A finished video is exciting, but the deeper win is the process. Kids learn that a strong story starts with a plan, that short scenes are easier to direct, that AI output needs human review, and that honest creators explain their tools.

Mistakes become clues. Revision becomes normal. Creativity becomes connected to consent, accuracy, and care.

Ace would call that a reason to celebrate:

> “Big ideas start with curiosity. Let’s build it!”
